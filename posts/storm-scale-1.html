Storming the Scale, Part 1

Magic mechanics are often talked about being placed on the 'storm scale'. This is a scale that was invented by Mark Rosewater to discuss how likely a mechanic is to return as a premier set mechanic. Today I'd like to rate all the custom mechanics that have been used in Many Worlds sets. I won't be rating mechanics that have appeared in the official game.

If you need a refresher on what some of these mechanics do, you can check out the rules here.

What is the storm scale?

The storm scale is a scale from 1-10 that gives whether a mechanic is likely to return a numeric value. Here is the scale I'll be using today:

Rating Meaning
1 Evergreen mechanic
2 Deciduous mechanic
3 Will most likely do again
4 Will likely do again, though there are problems
5 Will do again in the right place
6 May do again in the right place
7 Unlikely, unless the perfect set up exists
8 Very unlikely, unless the perfect set up exists
9 Not likely
10 No

I will also discuss the popularity of mechanics, though this is slightly skewed by my perception and the small playerbase.

Popularity Rating
Very popular People have expressed excitement about the mechanic, and cards that use the mechanic.
Somewhat popular People have showed interest in using the mechanic, or cards that use the mechanic.
Average Although the mechanic isn't exciting, it is generally useful.
Not popular Nobody liked the mechanic, and cards don't see much play.

Kingdom of Favola

Come Alive

Popularity: Somewhat popular

A common issue with auras comes when the creature they're attached to dies - the aura also goes to the graveyard, so you lose two cards in one. People liked that the mechanic bypassed that disadvantage.

Power Level: Average

An issue endemic to all mechanics in the Favola set, the cards that used the mechanic tended to perform below average.

Complexity: Average

As a DFC mechanic, it uses a certain amount of set complexity to include DFCs in a set, and the comprehension complexity is higher than usual, as a player needs to know the words on both sides of the card. In general, the effect on the back mirrored the effect on the front face, alleviating that slightly.

Storm Rating: 5


Outflank

Popularity: Somewhat popular

As a combat mechanic, particularly one that can grant evasion, it has seen some play in decks that are trying to gain value from combat triggers. The cards that see the most play are the ones that can grant outflank to more than one creature, simplifying set up.

Power Level: Average

Like come alive, the cards that use the mechanic are middling, though the mechanic gets stronger the better the combat damage triggers available to it are.

Complexity: Low

The mechanic is easy to understand and doesn't require a significant amount of set complexity. It does have some strategic complexity.

Storm Rating: 3


Spellbind

Popularity: Average

As a workhorse mechanic, spellbind was not meant to be splashy, but rather work as a pay-off for playing some of the plethora of auras and sagas in the set. That being said, enchantment decks tend to shy away from spellbind in favor of more immediate value from other mechanics like constellation.

Power Level: Low

Many of the cards with spellbind lacked the punch needed to bring the mechanic into its own, so they often lack board presence.

Complexity: Low

While needing to track whether you control three enchantments adds a little board complexity, overall, the mechanic is easy to fit into enchantment heavy environments, and is easy to understand.

Storm Rating: 3


Chromatopia

Artifact Mana

Popularity: Not popular

Due to their difficulty to use, cards with these mana symbols in their mana cost see no play. The mechanic is also not particularly exciting.

Power Level: Low

The mechanic being in the mana cost of a card led to too many games with that card being dead in hand, making the cards being very difficult to include even in decks dedicated to the corresponding artifact type.

Complexity: Average

The mechanic had some memory issues, since each artifact type needed its own symbol that needed to be memorised.

Storm Rating: 9 (6 for treasure mana)


Defiant

Popularity: Somewhat popular

As a repeatable removal mechanic, defiant has seen some play, usually on creatures that are otherwise already well costed and statted.

Power Level: High

The power of having a large defiant creature on board caused some problems in limited and low power environments. Due to this, the mechanic has been errata'd to include a number in its definition.

Complexity: Low

Although the mechanic itself doesn't add much complexity, like with all triggered abilities, it can add some memory issues when there are complex board states.

Storm Rating: 5


Paint, Color Counters, and Allegiance

Popularity: Average

The mechanic was novel, though somewhat difficult to use effectively. This was in part due to it being a very parasitic mechanic - it doesn't play well with cards from other sets. Paint was the most well received mechanic of the three, adding a one-time way of levelling up a creature.

Power Level: High

Allegiance in particular had some power balance issues, most notably surrounding the land Achroa, Source of All, which could produce vast amounts of mana when combined with cheap cards that entered with lots of color counters on.

Complexity: High

The existence of color counters introduce a large amount of both set and board complexity, as the color of each counter needs to be tracked. This prevents the majority of other counter types from appearing alongside them (the only other counter that appears in Chromatopia is the loyalty counter). Allegiance also requires you to track the number of color counters of specific colors, further adding to the board complexity.

Storm Rating: 10


Repress

Popularity: Not popular

Repress was not a particularly splashy mechanic, and was difficult to build around, as it triggered on your opponent's actions, making it difficult to build decks that utilise the effects.

Power Level: Low

In low power formats, it was very difficult to trigger repress, as the opponent rarely drew multiple cards in a turn, and forcing them to draw additional cards ended up with you further behind.

Complexity: Average

In limited in particular, repress, alongside revolt, another ability word in the set, were difficult to distinguish at a glance, leading to some mispicks.

Storm Rating: 8


Tales from the Abyss: Adventures in Nautilus

Barter

Popularity: Somewhat popular

Another workhorse mechanic that grants a little boost to spells. As it can sacrifice creatures, it has seen some play in sacrifice decks.

Power Level: Average

As the cost of sacrificing a permanent is nontrivial, the payoffs for bartering spells generally matched this cost, leading to it being a fairly balanced mechanic.

Complexity: Low

The mechanic doesn't contribute much complexity anywhere.

Storm Rating: 3


Draconic

Popularity: Somewhat popular

Being rewarded for playing big splashy spells is a core tenet of the power gamer archetype, so draconic sees some play in decks that already want to do this, in particular dragons.

Power Level: Low

Within Tales from the Abyss, there weren't many spells that used discount mechanics like affinity or alternate casting costs, so the mechanic was kept relatively fair. The power of the mechanic goes up drastically in larger formats that can make use of the triggers without needing to spend 5 mana every time.

Complexity: Low

As an ability word that only triggers off casting large spells, it doesn't contribute much complexity, except when spells are discounted.

Storm Rating: 5


Salvage

Popularity: Average

The popularity of salvage was gated behind the ability to understand what the mechanic actually did. Those who did understand had some success building some interesting combos and voltron strategies.

Power Level: Low

The requirement for the equipment to have Equip {2} meant some designs had to be toned down, leading to few breakthrough designs.

Complexity: High

Salvage is in the running for the mechanic with the most reminder text, trying to explain an overcomplicated effect that could've been expressed in a number of other ways much more intuitively, such as the use of equipment creatures or DFCs.

Storm Rating: 10


Wilds of Garrem

Chilltouch

Popularity: Average

There have been some individual cards with chilltouch that have sparked interest, but as stun counters don't have much support, the mechanic has not made much of an impact.

Power Level: Low

As it prevents the creature from dealing lethal damage to another creature, it is quite a situational mechanic.

Complexity: Average

Since the mechanic uses stun counters, it can only appear in sets that include those. It also has some comprehension complexity, in particular understanding that creatures dealt damage in this way won't die.

Storm Rating: 2


Enhance

Popularity: Average

Individual enhance cards have seen some play due to their utility.

Power Level: Average

Modal spells come with an innate power of versatility, meaning that enhance cards are rarely dead in your hand.

Complexity: Average

Some enhance spells have some strategic complexity, due to the way their keyword counters may interact with other abilities. The type of counter enhance can use is also dependent on the set, though the mechanic is templated to work with both +1/+1 and -1/-1 counters (along with any other type of counter that comes along) if a set needs it.

Storm Rating: 3

The mechanic has a lot of unexplored design space using different kinds of counters for different kinds of design.


Fiery

Popularity: Somewhat popular

Having an opposite to snow was seen as novel, and players liked the themes that fiery was built around.

Power Level: Average

The mechanic itself doesn't have any rules attached to it, but many fiery cards cared about controlling fiery permanents, which was generally trivial outside of limited thanks to basic fiery lands.

Complexity: Average

Some effects require you to count the number of fiery permanents you control, adding a little board complexity, and sets will generally only use mechanics like fiery in large quantities, making it difficult to splash in other sets.

Storm Rating: 4


Pinnacle

Popularity: Very popular

Pinnacle introduced the mystical subtype to the game, which went down very well with players. Pinnacle in particular was loved due to its ability, especially in commander, to let you cast a spell with a ridiculously high potency.

Power Level: High

Some of the pinnacle costs were misjudged, meaning that in both limited and commander, you can likely cast your spells with much stronger effects than the mana would suggest.

Complexity: Average

Reading the highest power among creatures on the battlefield is relatively simple, though mystical spells have some unusual edge cases that can make interactions slightly unintuitive.

Storm Rating: 3 (2 for mystical)


Shift and Unshift

Popularity: Not popular

Shift was a mechanic that had artificial complexity and tracking issues that made it very difficult to play with.

Power Level: Average

The back faces of many shift cards were able to have slightly higher stat lines than usual, though this usually only persisted through your turn.

Complexity: High

Shift comes with tracking issues, requiring you to transform every shift card when you play a land, but also when an opponent plays a land; the latter which usually doesn't pass priority.

Storm Rating: 8


Shinkara

Climax

Popularity: Very popular

Climax spells had very splashy effects, and were well received by players. The main criticism was that Shinkara didn't feature enough cards with the mechanic.

Power Level: High

As a mechanic you can only use once per game, it was able to push the boundaries of power level.

Complexity: Average

Once you've climaxed a spell, a reminder marker is generally required to help you remember not to climax another spell during a game.

Storm Rating: 4


Hero Role

Popularity: Not popular

Having an aura that doesn't offer any immediate buff meant that the role token was rarely created, and when it was, rarely had an impact on the board.

Power Level: Low

The hero role only really shone when combined with another creature that already used power counters.

Complexity: High

For the hero role to function, power counters also need to be added to the creature (potentially by the hero role's effect), which can lead to messy board states.

Storm Rating: 8


Infiltrate

Popularity: Very popular

Players like drawing cards.

Power Level: High

Infiltrate, when combined with an evasive or unblockable creature can lead to a solid card draw engine.

Complexity: Low

The only complexity issue with the mechanic is the potential for missed triggers.

Storm Rating: 2


Superheroic

Popularity: Not popular

Superheroic was a wordy ability word, largely so that it was kept in check. The main draw of the mechanic is to target the creatures multiple times in a turn, which is difficult to do unless you build around the mechanic.

Power Level: Low

Card designs had to respect the possibility that a player might trigger superheroic an infinite number of times, so were generally designed to only trigger meaningfully once or twice, or triggers led to diminishing returns.

Complexity: High

The set needed a lot of ways for you to target your creatures, which added a decent amount of complexity to the set.

Storm Rating: 7
